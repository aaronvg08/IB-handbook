- **How does Offred’s perception of her body change in these chapters, particularly during the Ceremony and other interactions? How does Atwood use bodily imagery to explore themes of control and identity?**

Offred’s reflects about her own perception of her body throughout the chapters, using flashbacks as one of the main tools to express it. Before Gilead, Offred viewed her body just like any other woman at the time, and didn’t let it define who she was. However, as Offred goes through the Red Center and is indoctrinated with the Handmaid’s values, she now perceives what she used to wear as blasphemous. Like Atwood wrote:

> “Did I really wear bathing suits, at the beach? I did, without thought, among men,  
> without caring that my legs, my arms, my thighs and back were on display,  
> could be seen. _Shameful, immodest._ I avoid looking down at my body, not so  
> much because it’s shameful or immodest but because I don’t want to see it. I  
> don’t want to look at something that determines me so completely.”

Now, Offred’s identity has been reduced to her body and its utility. She feels her body ‘determines’ her. Atwood uses the word ‘on display’. This lets the reader know how Offred feels about her body now. Not only was her body shown, it was _on display_, like it’s intended for others to see and judge. These feelings of shame are used to control the Handmaids, from what they do to what they think. It’s easier to manipulate someone if they believe what they’re thinking is right.

  

- **How does Atwood depict the relationship between Serena Joy and Offred? How do their interactions in these chapters reveal the complexities of power and victimhood within Gilead?**

It’s easier to consider that Offred, as a Handmaid, immediately has less power than Serena Joy. It’s easy to think of her as a victim. However, it is revealed throughout the chapters, that the reality isn’t as simple as that. Atwood makes the lack of rapport between the two clear throughout the chapters; none of them respect each other.

The reader could interpret this as out of jealousy. After all, Serena Joy is unable to have children, which is why Offred is there to begin with. This is the main reason behind the tension between them. Serena Joy hates the fact that Offred has to be there, in the Ceremony. That her children won’t be truly hers. It could be argued that this biological superiority makes Offred more powerful than Serena Joy. They’re both victims of the circumstances and the system that has been forced upon them, despite having varying levels of control and power.

  

- **In these chapters, Offred reflects more on the former world, including her relationship with her daughter. How does the contrast between past and present enhances the novel’s critique of societal regression?**

Atwood introduces the past through flashbacks that come to Offred. It communicates to the viewer how things have drastically changed in society. Offred’s reflection on the past is firstly used as a tool to let the reader know what’s considered normal or appropriate in the present. Her perception of her body, her name, other people, media, censorship, freedom, and identity, among other topics.

It’s also used to deepen the novel’s critique of societal regression. The change didn’t take place from one day to another; it took time. Slowly but surely laws were taken away, rights that women used to enjoy. The story is written in such a way that it makes sense for it to happen in the real world. What Atwood calls “speculative fiction”. It _could_ happen to _you_. Not only that, but Atwood’s self-imposed rule of not making any events up deepens this feeling. The book’s critique is especially clever since it divulges into real-life contexts, picking and sorting them to solidify the horrific scenario that it presents.
