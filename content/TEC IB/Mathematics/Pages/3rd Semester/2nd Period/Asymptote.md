An asymptote is a phenomenon that happens in [[Rational function|rational functions]] where the solutions tend to zero in a division and then turn towards positive or negative infinity ($\infty$ and $-\infty$ respectively) since you cannot divide by zero. Asymptotes can come in three flavors:

- Vertical asymptotes.
- Horizontal asymptotes.
- Oblique asymptote (rare).

# Vertical asymptote

To get the vertical asymptote in a rational function, you equal the denominator of the function to zero. So, for this function:

$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{x+3}
$$
You do:

$$
x+3=0
$$
$$
x=-3
$$

Therefore, the point of $x$ where there is an asymptote is 3. 

# Horizontal asymptote

To get the vertical asymptote, you have to evaluate whether or not it exists in the first place. If a horizontal asymptote doesn't exist, it can mean many things:

- It literally doesn't exist.
- There are infinite horizontal asymptotes.

Depending on the case. To know whether or not it exists, you can consider the numerator and denominator of a [[Rational function|rational function]]. So, for a function that looks like this:

$$
f(x)=\frac{numerator}{denominator}
$$

Check the leading exponent for each side of the fraction, so, the highest exponent on an $x$. Then, check the following cases:

- When $numerator>denominator$, there is no asymptote.
- When $numerator<denominator$, the asymptote is $y=0$.
- When $numerator=denominator$, it is the result of the division of the leading coefficients, meaning, the number next to the $x$ with the highest exponents.
