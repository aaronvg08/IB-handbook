Indefinite integrals are a type of [[Integral|integral]] that are called indefinite because they give us infinite functions as a result. The reason for this is because a function can have many constants that disappear during [[Derivative|differentiation]], which is why without information we cannot know the exact value of this constant.

So, we add a $+C$ to compensate for this. This is called the constant of integration, and a normal integration can look something like this.

$$
\int \frac{x^{2}}{2} \, dx=x+C
$$

# Rules

Just like when differentiating, there are rules for integration. Derivatives share a rule with the integral, but there are also some extra special ones for integration.

- [[Power rule]]
- [[Constant rule]]
- [[Reciprocal rule]]
- [[Euler's exponent rule]]

# Boundaries

The integral of something is indefinite unless we have a boundary condition (or an initial condition). For example, the integral of $4x+3$ is the following.

$$
\int 4x+3 \, dx=2x^{2}+3x+C 
$$

With a boundary condition, we can find $C$. Say that $f(1)=9$. Then, we do the following.

$$
f(1)=2(1)^{2}+3(1)+C
$$
$$
2(1)^{2}+3(1)+C=9
$$
$$
2+3+C=9
$$
$$
C=9-5
$$
$$
C=4
$$

So know we know the one and only function and not infinite functions that may be the original one. It is the following.

$$
f(x)=2x^{2}+3x+4
$$
