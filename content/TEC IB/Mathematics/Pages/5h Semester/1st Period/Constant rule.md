The constant rule in [[Calculus|calculus]] is an important rule to know when it comes to [[Derivative|differentiating]] and [[Integral|integrating]] [[Function|functions]]. 

# Derivative

It really is two rules, which state that the derivative of a constant is always zero, and that a constant multiple can be ignored while the insides are differentiated.

$$
f(x)=C,\;\;\text{if}\;C\in \mathbb{R},\;\;f'(x)=0
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}[C\cdot f(x)]=C(f'(x)),\;\;\text{provided }f'(x)\text{ exists}
$$

>[!note]
>The first property of this rule is simply just a consequence of the [[Power rule|power rule]], as seen below.
>$\frac{d}{dx}[5]=\frac{d}{dx}[5\cdot x^{0}]$
>$=5\cdot 0 \cdot  x^{-1}$
>$=0$

# Integral

The [[Indefinite integral|indefinite integral]] of a constant is simply that constant multiplied by $x$.

$$
\int k \, dx =kx+C
$$
