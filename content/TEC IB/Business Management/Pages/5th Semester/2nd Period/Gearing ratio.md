The gearing ratio is an [[Efficiency ratios|efficiency ratio]] that measures how much of a business' capital employed is financed by long-term debt, such as non-current [[Liabilities|liabilities]]. The higher the ratio, the more debt there is embedded into the business. The formula for this can be found below.

$$
\text{Gearing ratio}=\frac{\text{Non-current liabilities}}{\text{Capital employed}}\times 100
$$
$$
\text{Capital employed}=\text{Non-current liabilities}+ \text{Equity}
$$

>[!note]
>A gearing ratio of 25% is generally considered low. A gearing ratio of 25% to 50% would be considered normal. A gearing ratio above 50% is considered high.

There are some strategies to improve the gearing ratio if it is unsatisfactory:

- **Paying off liabilities**: Paying their long-term liabilities lowers the ratio, but it can be costly and reduce sales revenue.
- **Increase retained profit**: Earn more and have that balance how much of the business is based on debt. However, this could mean reducing dividends, which could make [[Stakeholders|shareholders]] unhappy.
- **Selling more shares**: This will increase the denominator and consequently decrease the gearing ratio. The value of equity will increase. However, you start loosing control of the company and start owning less and less of it.
