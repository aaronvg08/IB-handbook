Market research is the process of gathering information to help with [[Marketing planning|marketing decisions]]. Organizations carry out market research in order to gain a better understanding of their customers, competitors and markets.

The main reasons why a business carries out market research are the following.

- Identifying consumers’ needs and wants as well as their behavior.
- Identifying potential changes in the market.
- Testing new products.
- Evaluating existing marketing efforts in attracting consumers.
- Investigating new possibilities in existing or new markets.

>[!note]
>[[Social enterprise|Social enterprises]] also carry out market research to identify human needs.

# Types of research

- **Product-based**: A company releases a product to a specific area for a limited time. This enables the company to gain large amounts of information about how closely the product meets the needs of its target market.
- **Price-based**: Companies study the prices that other companies have for their products, and adjust accordingly to ensure that they are in line with the competition.
- **Promotion-based**: Companies research and understand which media their target market accesses and reacts to.
- **Place-based**: A company conducts research to select the right distribution channels for a product. It can be the difference between success and failure.
- **People-based**: Businesses research what kind of people they would need to offer the experience they are aiming to give.
- **Process-based**: Companies research how to best give a service to its customers, and what processes this implies.
- **Physical-evidence-based**: This involves researching the sensory and visual experiences of the customer. This might include branding, the layout of the store or the store’s ambience..

>[!note]
>This has to do with the elements of the [[Marketing mix|marketing mix]]!

## Primary research

Primary market research involves creating new information and aims to answer a specific research question. The information it produces is up to date and relevant. The business can find out about the individuals’ tastes and preferences, the reasons why they make purchases, or whether they like the product.

- **Surveys**: Can include sent by mail or done face-to-face. They are well-suited for gathering quantitative data rather than qualitative data. A questionnaire is not the same thing as a survey. A questionnaire refers only to the form that is distributed and completed. A survey is the process, a questionnaire is the method used to carry out the survey.
- **Interviews**: It is a lengthier surveying method and allows researchers to gain large amounts of qualitative data. They can be conducted face-to-face, over the telephone or through video conferencing. Interviews allow researchers to ask follow-up questions, so it is possible for the interviewer to get more detail on an idea or opinion.
- **Focus groups**: An interview conducted with a small group of individuals, usually with similar characteristics. Participants may be offered some sort of financial reward for taking part. The researcher may organize the setting and the questions, or they may simply lead the conversation in a particular direction.
- **Observations**: Observations allow for natural reactions of customers to be studied. Individual consumers can be followed around the store and details such as the route they take and which promotions they notice are recorded. This can help managers design more effective layouts for their stores. 

| Uses                                                                 | Limitations                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Provides direct information about customers’ tastes and preferences. | Can be expensive and time-consuming to carry out.                  |
| Provides information about reasons for purchases.                    | Staff may need [[Training\|training]] to carry out the research.   |
| Provides unique information that can give a competitive advantage.   | Can be difficult to construct effective questions and experiments. |


## Secondary research

Secondary market research uses information gathered by others. This means that it can be carried out rapidly and can give a quick overview of a market and can also be used to learn about whether the economy of an area is growing, or what the major sociocultural trends are.

- **Market analyses**: Companies can either pay a market research agency to carry out new research, or they can purchase a market analysis report that has already been published. Paying for new research will give the business the most up-to-date information, but it's expensive, unlike buying market analysis reports that have already been published.
- **Academic journals**: They contain articles on new research and academic theory. The articles are published by academics from leading universities. Many business management theories, such as [[Maslow's hierarchy of needs|Maslow’s hierarchy of needs]] or the [[Boston consulting group (BGC) matrix|BGC matrix]], were first published in academic journals.
- **Government publications**: Governments from all over the world regularly publish data covering topics such as population statistics and economic conditions. This data is normally considered reliable and up to date and are released for free and are updated regularly.
- **Media articles**: They are published in both printed and electronic newspapers and magazines. They are updated regularly and provide current local and/or national information.
- **Other online content**: The internet is a big place, and there are other sources out there. However, you should consider the reliability, relevance, accuracy, and purpose of the data before using it.

| Uses                                                                                           | Limitations                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provides information at lower cost than primary research.                                      | The business must rely on the research methods of others.       |
| Provides broader contextual information about the whole economy, population or general trends. | Information that the business wants may not exist.              |
| Published information is often already available.                                              | Existing information may not be fit for the business’s purpose. |
